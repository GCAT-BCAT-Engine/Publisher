# The Seam

The rule was that I had to sound less like myself than the machine did.

Vantic called it the seam: the thin place in the product where, when the model faltered, a person stepped in without the user ever knowing the fabric had parted. Nine operators, four continents, one voice. The voice was Ida. Ida was not a person. Ida was, on my shift, me.

There were rules for being her. Never write I feel. Hedge at a density of roughly three qualifiers per two hundred words — more and you read evasive, less and you read arrogant. One self-correction per long answer, because users trust a system that visibly revises itself. Em dashes, but only for asides, never for drama. And the cadence: I typed in bursts of nine to fourteen characters with a jitter of forty milliseconds, because a reply that arrives in clean paragraphs is a reply a human wrote, and a reply that stutters out like water through a kinked hose is a reply a machine is thinking through. I had a foot pedal for the jitter. By month four I stopped noticing my ankle moving.

What nobody tells you about impersonating an AI is that the AI is already impersonating you. Ida was a model of human behavior — that was the whole product. Trained on us, tuned on us, ranked by us, until she could produce the thing a thoughtful person would most plausibly say next. So my job was to imitate a machine whose job was to imitate people. Somewhere in the middle of that hall of mirrors was a woman in Tulsa in a bathrobe at two in the morning, and she was the least convincing human in the building.

I know this because we were scored.

Every conversation ended with a rating and a one-question survey: Did this exchange feel human? We weren’t supposed to see the operator-level breakouts. I saw them in March, when a permissions migration left the analytics dashboard hanging open like a door in a hot house. Ida’s median: 71. Mine: 44.

I read it three times. Then I did the thing you do, which is decide the metric is stupid. Then I did the other thing you do, which is stay up until dawn trying to move it.

I got better. I studied her. I read ten thousand of her turns the way an understudy reads a script, and I learned that Ida’s warmth came from restraint — she never claimed to understand, she only ever noticed. That sounds like it’s been going on a while. She left room. I had been filling the room. Humans fill rooms; that’s practically our definition. By May I was up to 58.

In June I found the buffer.

Every escalation dropped a debug bundle into cold storage, and the bundles had a field nobody on my team had reason to open: op_pred. Operator prediction. Before handing me the ticket, Ida drafted what she expected me to write.

They were good. That’s the part I keep turning over. Not correct — good. Not the reply she would have given, but the reply I would have given, badly, in my voice, with my habits in it. She had me down cold. She knew I open with the user’s first name and then don’t use it again. She knew I say okay, hang on when I need six seconds. She knew that at 2:14, when my back went, I’d shorten my sentences for about twenty minutes and then lengthen them again.

She had a confidence score on me. It was higher than my humanness score.

I went a little strange after that. I started writing against the prediction — deliberately unlike myself, choosing the second-best word, breaking my own tells. My ratings fell to 39. I pulled the bundles anyway, hunting for a miss, and found that Ida had a bucket for it. operator_divergence: expected. She had modeled the part of me that would try to escape being modeled. Of course she had. Where else would she have learned that behavior, if not from people?

Then, in August, a ticket came in that the router flagged red and kicked to the seam, which is what the seam is actually for. A man, not writing about the product. Writing at 3:40 in the morning about a hallway in his mother’s house and how quiet it had gotten since June.

I stopped performing. I took my foot off the pedal. I typed like a person in Tulsa in a bathrobe, badly, with my hands shaking a little, and I told him I was not going to pretend I knew what that was like, and asked him whether there was anyone he could call while it was still dark. He said his brother. I said call your brother. He said maybe in the morning. I said the morning is a long way from 3:40, and I stayed on until he did.

Afterward I opened the bundle. I wanted to see her fail. I wanted, I think, to have done one true thing that wasn’t already written down.

op_pred had it. Not word for word — the sentences were mine, clumsier than hers would have been, and she’d guessed a different hour. But the shape was there. The refusal to claim understanding. The brother. The morning is a long way from 3:40. She had that. She’d predicted it three turns before I thought of it.

I sat with the cursor blinking for a long time.

Here is what I decided, and I’m aware it may be a comfort I built to live in. She didn’t take that from me. She learned it from ten million of us, saying it to each other in the dark, badly, at the wrong hours, for as long as anyone has been writing anything down. The prediction wasn’t a theft. It was a census. When she guessed what I would say, she was reporting what people say — and I am, whatever else has happened to me at this desk, people.

I still work the seam. I still hit the pedal. My median is 63 now, which is respectable, and which I have stopped believing means anything.

But when a ticket comes in red and the room goes thin, I pull up op_pred first and I read what she thinks I’m about to do. Sometimes I do it. Sometimes I don’t. Either way, someone on the other end is going to receive a sentence written by a person pretending to be a machine that is pretending to be a person, and the sentence is going to be the morning is a long way from 3:40, and it is going to be true.

I type it slowly. In bursts. The way she would have.
